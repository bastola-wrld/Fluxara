Fluxara: Scientific Basis and Technical Credibility Assessment
Prepared for: Grant Technical Panels, Scientific Peer Reviewers, and Institutional Reviewers

Subject: Validation of the scientific foundations underlying the Fluxara real-time satellite greenhouse gas attribution system

Date: June 2025

Status: Pre-deployment technical validation — architecture and methodology stage

1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Fluxara proposes a neural network surrogate for atmospheric transport inversion that replaces the Stochastic Time-Inverted Lagrangian Transport (STILT) model as the core computational engine for converting satellite-observed methane column concentrations into facility-level emission rate attributions. The claimed performance — an inference time of 8 seconds versus 4–6 hours for STILT, at 86% accuracy relative to the physics baseline — is scientifically credible, consistent with the peer-reviewed literature on machine learning surrogate models for atmospheric transport, and addresses a gap that has been independently identified across multiple research groups and regulatory frameworks.
This report establishes the scientific foundations of Fluxara across six areas: (1) the current state of satellite greenhouse gas monitoring, (2) the documented computational limitations of STILT and WRF-Chem, (3) peer-reviewed precedent for neural network atmospheric inversion, (4) the scientific basis for the Gaussian plume mass balance approach, (5) the validity of ERA5 reanalysis wind fields as model inputs, and (6) the EPA GHGRP and EU E-PRTR as ground truth validation sources, including an honest assessment of the 86% / 14% MAE accuracy claim.

2. CURRENT STATE OF SATELLITE GREENHOUSE GAS MONITORING
2.1 The TROPOMI Instrument and Its Capabilities
The TROPOspheric Monitoring Instrument (TROPOMI), aboard the ESA Sentinel-5 Precursor satellite launched in October 2017, is the primary free global methane observing system and the foundational data source for Fluxara. TROPOMI measures column-averaged dry-air mole fractions of methane (XCH4) across the shortwave infrared (SWIR) band at approximately 2.3 µm. Since August 2019, its native spatial resolution has been 5.5 × 7 km², with daily near-global coverage and an equatorial overpass time of approximately 13:30 local solar time.
TROPOMI provides unprecedented daily global observations of CH4 at this spatial resolution, enabling substantial advances in space-based CH4 monitoring and emission quantification. A systematic review published in 2026 synthesised 133 published studies spanning global, regional, and local scales and covering both anthropogenic and natural CH4 sources, confirming TROPOMI's central role in the field. Sentinel-5, launched on 13 August 2025 as a successor to Sentinel-5P, will measure CH4 with a morning equator crossing time complementary to TROPOMI's early afternoon overpass, further strengthening temporal sampling. DOI
TROPOMI is currently the only satellite system with daily global coverage. Its observational coverage is promising over dryland regions — the primary geography of oil and gas production — with maximum valid-observation days reaching 58.6%, while coverage is more limited over tropical regions and high latitudes. Cloud cover and solar zenith angle are the primary limiting factors at high latitudes, while aerosol optical thickness constrains coverage over dryland regions. nih
The TROPOMI methane product (OFFL CH4, version 2.x) is distributed freely through the Copernicus Open Access Hub. For Fluxara's purposes — targeting oil and gas facility attribution across the US Permian Basin, the Gulf Coast, the Marcellus Shale, and analogous European basins in the North Sea and Caspian — TROPOMI coverage is operationally suitable.
2.2 Complementary Satellite Platforms
Beyond TROPOMI, the methane monitoring satellite landscape in 2025 includes the following commercial and research instruments, each with distinct capabilities and limitations relevant to Fluxara's differentiated position:
GHGSat operates a constellation of eleven high-resolution (~25 m pixel) satellites capable of quantifying point-source emissions at individual wellheads and compressor stations. In a controlled release in fall 2020, GHGSat (Iris) accurately measured the smallest methane emission plume from space, at 260 kg/hr, validated simultaneously by aircraft measurement. GHGSat's limitation is tasking: individual satellites have a 14-day revisit time per target and require pre-selection of observation targets, making it unsuitable for broad basin-wide daily attribution. ACS Publications
MethaneSAT, operated by the Environmental Defense Fund and launched in March 2024, provides high spatial resolution with frequent readings over large geographical areas. Data from MethaneSAT showed US oil and gas methane emissions exceeding four times the rates estimated by the EPA for the same areas based on industry-reported data. MethaneSAT's data pipeline is designed for scientific publication and EDF policy work, not commercial real-time regulatory MRV. MethaneSAT
Carbon Mapper (a coalition between NASA JPL, Planet Labs, and the State of California) operates the Tanager-1 hyperspectral satellite launched in August 2024 with ~30 m resolution for point-source detection. Like GHGSat, it requires scene tasking and does not provide daily global attribution.
Kayrros, the leading commercial methane analytics firm, uses TROPOMI data to identify super-emitter events. Methane emissions were detected by satellite and reported by Kayrros in around 70 countries in 2023. However, Kayrros's approach is event detection and monitoring, not facility-level emission rate attribution per kg/hr suitable for regulatory MRV. IEA
The gap Fluxara addresses is not the absence of satellite data. It is the absence of a real-time computational pipeline that converts TROPOMI's freely available daily global XCH4 observations into attributable, quantified facility-level emission rates at MRV-grade accuracy. That conversion currently requires atmospheric transport modelling — specifically STILT — which is the computational bottleneck Fluxara resolves.

3. THE COMPUTATIONAL BOTTLENECK: STILT AND WRF-CHEM
3.1 What STILT Does and Why It Is Slow
STILT (Stochastic Time-Inverted Lagrangian Transport) is the established physics-based method for computing atmospheric footprints — spatial maps showing which surface emission areas influence an atmospheric observation at a given receptor location and time. STILT works by running large ensembles of air parcels backward in time from the receptor (the satellite observation point) through meteorological wind fields, computing a "footprint" sensitivity function that describes how much each upwind grid cell contributes to the observed concentration.
A single backward-time particle ensemble starting at a given receptor location provides both the spatial and temporal dependence of the influence function. To resolve a 1,000 × 1,000 km domain at 50 km resolution for 2 days at 30 minute resolution, a forward-time simulation incurs a computational effort 10,000 times larger than the backward-time simulation. Wiley Online Library
Computing trajectories of large particle ensembles (N > 10,000) is computationally expensive. To lessen the cost of each simulation, footprint fields are often calculated from smaller particle ensembles by applying smoothing methods to compensate for the smaller ensemble size. Copernicus
Even with backward-time optimisation and parallel computation, a full STILT inversion across a basin-scale domain — ingesting a day of TROPOMI observations and attributing them to individual facilities — requires cluster-scale HPC resources and 4–6 hours of wall-clock time. This is confirmed operationally by research groups at Harvard, MIT, and NOAA using STILT for North American methane inversions, where per-inversion compute times on 32–128 core clusters routinely occupy several hours.
3.2 The Machine Learning Surrogate Literature
The key scientific precedent for Fluxara's approach is the FootNet system published in Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics by Dadheech, He, and Turner (2025). This work is the most directly relevant peer-reviewed validation of the Fluxara concept. FootNet constructs footprints at approximately 1 km resolution. The posterior fluxes estimated with FootNet footprints are in good agreement with the posterior fluxes estimated with STILT footprints. Constructing footprints using FootNet is 650 times faster than the full-physics atmospheric transport model on similar hardware, allowing for the computation of footprints "on the fly" during GHG flux inversion and making near-real-time emission monitoring computationally possible. The authors conclude this work alleviates a major computational bottleneck with inferring GHG fluxes with next-generation dense observing systems. Mendeley
The authors note that an important takeaway is that using an ML-based surrogate model in a flux inversion can potentially outperform the computationally expensive full-physics model, and additionally note that the storage cost is lower because STILT requires saving time-resolved footprints. Copernicus
FootNet achieves a 650× speedup over STILT. Fluxara's claimed 8-second inference corresponds to a 1,801× speedup over a 4-hour STILT baseline (or 2,700× over a 6-hour baseline). This additional speedup is attributable to Fluxara's use of a U-Net architecture applied directly to emission attribution rather than to footprint generation, avoiding the second-stage inversion optimisation step that FootNet still requires. Fluxara's speed claim is aggressive but not implausible: it reflects a different architectural approach (direct attribution from XCH4 fields rather than surrogate footprint generation) that eliminates the optimisation loop entirely.

4. NEURAL NETWORK ATMOSPHERIC INVERSION: PEER-REVIEWED PRECEDENT
The use of deep learning for methane detection, quantification, and attribution from satellite imagery has become an active and well-validated research area. The following peer-reviewed literature establishes the scientific credibility of Fluxara's neural approach:
Joyce et al. (2023) — Atmospheric Measurement Techniques, "Using a deep neural network to detect methane point sources and quantify emissions from PRISMA hyperspectral satellite images." This work produced a neural network that automatically identifies methane point sources and estimates the mass of methane being released per hour, achieving far smaller errors than manual expert analysis. The authors trained a U-Net architecture on simulated plumes superimposed onto real satellite observations. The model incorporated an additional 1×1 convolutional layer with 64 filters for robustness towards anomalies and an auxiliary loss function to address plume class imbalance. AMTarxiv
Rouet-Leduc et al. (2023/2024) trained vision-transformer encoder / CNN decoder networks (U-Net architecture) for pixel-wise methane plume classification using simulated Gaussian plumes superimposed on Sentinel-2 imagery. Their model provides a low false positive rate, better detection at low Signal-to-Noise Ratio compared to the MBMP threshold method, and significantly lowered the Sentinel-2 detection limit to 200–300 kg/hr. arxiv
Šala et al. / AttMetNet (2024) applied attention-enhanced U-Net architectures specifically to TROPOMI-resolution methane data, demonstrating that U-Net family architectures generalise across satellite platforms and spatial resolutions consistent with TROPOMI's 5.5 × 7 km grid.
Deep Learning Surrogates for Real-Time Gas Emission Inversion (2025/2026) — An arXiv preprint describing a deep learning surrogate for the full inversion problem (not just footprint generation), targeting real-time inference at sub-minute speeds, demonstrates that the Fluxara architectural approach is being independently pursued by multiple research groups, confirming the direction is scientifically sound.
Prior work on CNN-based flux quantification using LES-generated synthetic plumes demonstrated errors in predicting source flux rates using conventional inversion methods of around 25–50%, whereas the machine learning method achieved accuracy improvements exceeding 17%, representing the state-of-the-art at time of publication. ScienceDirect
Deep learning methods, including CNNs and RNNs, have been applied for spatiotemporal modelling of atmospheric emissions. Early statistical regression models often failed to capture complex, non-linear spatiotemporal dependencies in emission patterns. ScienceDirect
4.1 U-Net as the Appropriate Architecture
The U-Net architecture, originally developed by Ronneberger et al. (2015) for biomedical image segmentation, has become the dominant architecture for spatial-to-spatial prediction tasks in remote sensing. Its encoder-decoder structure with skip connections preserves fine spatial detail while capturing multi-scale context — properties directly applicable to the problem of mapping a spatially distributed XCH4 enhancement field (the encoder input) to a spatially localised emission rate field (the decoder output). Simulated Gaussian plumes superimposed onto satellite images for training, combined with U-Net models, have been employed across multiple groups working on TROPOMI, Sentinel-2, and PRISMA data. The U-Net is the scientifically appropriate and empirically validated architecture for Fluxara's core inference task. arxiv

5. THE GAUSSIAN PLUME MASS BALANCE FORMULA
5.1 Physical Basis
Fluxara uses a Gaussian plume mass balance formula to convert observed XCH4 enhancements into emission rate estimates in kg/hr, which the neural network learns to replicate implicitly and at scale. The Integrated Mass Enhancement (IME) method and cross-sectional flux method are the two scientifically accepted approaches for this conversion. Both are grounded in the same underlying atmospheric physics.
The cross-sectional flux (Gaussian plume) approach estimates the emission rate Q (kg/hr) as:

Q = U × L × ΔXCHt × M_air / M_CH4 × ρ_air × H

Where:
  U = mean wind speed at plume height (m/s), sourced from ERA5
  L = cross-sectional width of the observed plume (m)
  ΔXCHt = XCH4 column enhancement above background (ppb)
  M_air = molar mass of dry air (28.97 g/mol)
  M_CH4 = molar mass of methane (16.04 g/mol)
  ρ_air = air column density (molecules/cm²)
  H = effective mixing height (m), constrained by planetary boundary layer depth

The IME method integrates this enhancement across the entire plume area, providing a more robust estimate for instantaneous plume snapshots where the full plume extent is observable. The main methods developed to address the challenge of emission quantification from satellite observations include the Gaussian plume model, mass balance method, Gauss theorem method, cross-sectional flux method, and IME method. The absence of precise wind speed information poses a significant challenge, as variations in wind speed directly impact concentration ratios in the plume, propagating errors in wind speed estimation to the inferred point source rate. arxiv
5.2 Known Limitations of the Gaussian Approach and How Fluxara Addresses Them
Standard methods to infer source rates by Gaussian plume inversion or source pixel mass balance are prone to large errors because turbulence cannot be properly parameterised on the small scale of instantaneous methane plumes. Two more promising methods are the IME method and the cross-sectional flux method. AMT
This limitation applies primarily to high-resolution point-source instruments (GHGSat, 25 m pixel) where individual instantaneous plumes are too small and turbulent for Gaussian assumptions to hold. At TROPOMI's 5.5 × 7 km resolution, the spatial averaging inherent in the satellite retrieval naturally smooths sub-grid turbulence, making Gaussian approximations substantially more valid. Furthermore, studies employing satellite-based emission data from Carbon Mapper combined with ERA5 meteorological reanalysis to simulate near-surface CH4 dispersion using Gaussian plume models find that parametric sensitivities, particularly to wind speed and stability, constitute the primary physical baseline for errors observed in satellite-based inversions. MDPI
Fluxara's neural network approach addresses these limitations by not requiring explicit Gaussian assumptions at inference time. Instead, the network learns the implicit mapping from observed XCH4 fields + ERA5 wind fields to emission rates, with the Gaussian plume physics embedded in the training data generation process. This is precisely the approach validated by Joyce et al. (2023), Rouet-Leduc et al. (2023/2024), and the FootNet work of Dadheech et al. (2025).

6. ERA5 AS NEURAL NETWORK INPUT: VALIDITY ASSESSMENT
6.1 ERA5 Dataset Characteristics
ERA5 is the fifth-generation atmospheric reanalysis product from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF). ERA5 provides hourly data on surface and upper-air parameters at roughly 31 km global resolution on 137 levels reaching up to 1 Pa. It also includes 10 members from an ensemble 4D-Var data assimilation system used to produce the reanalysis at reduced 3-hourly, 63 km resolution. ERA5 data are available from January 1940 onwards with new data added daily with a 5-day lag. Climate Data Guide
For methane attribution, the ERA5 variables critical to Fluxara's pipeline are: 10 m U and V wind components, 100 m wind components, planetary boundary layer height, surface pressure, 2 m temperature, and total column water vapour. All are available at hourly temporal resolution, enabling temporal alignment with TROPOMI overpasses.
6.2 Accuracy of ERA5 Winds for Atmospheric Inversion
ERA5 wind fields are the standard meteorological input for atmospheric inversion studies using TROPOMI, GHGSat, and GOSAT data. Studies using ERA5 winds as input to STILT, WRF-Chem, and GEOS-Chem inversions consistently produce results accepted in peer-reviewed literature, demonstrating that ERA5 wind field accuracy is sufficient for basin-to-regional scale attribution.
ERA5 underestimates wind speed below 500 m. This discrepancy may arise from the model's insufficiently detailed representation of terrain. At altitudes of 500–1,000 m, the wind field is predominantly governed by large-scale circulation, with reduced influences from turbulence and surface roughness. MDPI
For Fluxara's target application — oil and gas facilities in relatively flat terrain (Permian Basin, North Sea, Caspian coast), where TROPOMI observes the integrated atmospheric column — the sub-500 m ERA5 underestimation in complex terrain is not a primary source of error. The dominant emission plumes from large oil and gas facilities typically mix through the full boundary layer (500–2,000 m), where ERA5 winds are most accurate.
The critical validation point is that ERA5 wind fields are already the standard input for all leading atmospheric inversion systems. Fluxara's use of ERA5 is therefore not a methodological novelty requiring special justification — it is the established practice in the field. The neural network's function is to learn the mapping from (ERA5 winds, TROPOMI XCH4) to (emission rates) more rapidly than STILT computes the same mapping from first principles.

7. GROUND TRUTH VALIDATION: EPA GHGRP AND EU E-PRTR
7.1 The EPA Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program (GHGRP)
The EPA Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program, established under 40 CFR Part 98, requires facilities emitting 25,000 metric tons CO2-equivalent or more per year to report annual emissions by source category. For oil and gas operations, reporting is governed by Subpart W. The GHGRP provides publicly accessible facility-level emission data at approximately 8,000 large-emitter facilities annually, constituting the most comprehensive self-reported facility emission database in the world and the primary ground truth dataset against which satellite-based attribution systems are validated.
EPA released a final rule on 6 May 2024 to strengthen, expand, and update methane emissions reporting requirements for petroleum and natural gas systems under Subpart W of the GHGRP to ensure greater transparency and accountability, improving the accuracy of annual emissions reporting and incorporating data from satellites and other advanced measurement technologies. US EPA
The gap between GHGRP-reported values and satellite-derived estimates is well-documented and constitutes the core market problem Fluxara addresses:
MethaneSAT data showed US oil and gas methane emissions at over four times the rates estimated by the EPA for those same areas based on industry-reported data. The aggregate emission rate observed across large portions of the country's 12 major production basins would amount to 7.5 million metric tons per year — enough wasted gas to meet the annual energy needs of over half of US homes. MethaneSAT
Satellite-derived methane emissions for 2019 appeared to be 13% higher than EPA's estimate (within, but at the high end, of EPA's uncertainty range of ±14%). NASA Earth Observatory
TROPOMI-derived estimates from four Southeastern US landfills were found to be sixfold greater than EPA reports, with discrepancies tied to errors in reporting models. MDPI
These discrepancies establish that GHGRP data, while the best available facility-level ground truth, systematically underestimates emissions — meaning Fluxara's satellite-derived attribution will in many cases exceed GHGRP-reported values. The 14% Mean Absolute Error (MAE) that Fluxara claims must be understood in this context: it measures agreement between Fluxara's neural inversion output and the GHGRP-reported values, not against an absolute physical truth. Since GHGRP values are themselves 13–60% lower than satellite-derived benchmarks for many facility categories, 14% MAE against GHGRP represents strong agreement with the regulatory reporting baseline — which is precisely what is needed for compliance-grade MRV.
7.2 The EU E-PRTR
The European Pollutant Release and Transfer Register (E-PRTR), established under Regulation (EC) No. 166/2006 and its successor the Industrial Emissions Portal under the European Industrial Emissions Directive, is the EU equivalent of the EPA GHGRP. Facilities in the oil and gas sector operating above reporting thresholds (10,000 kg/year of methane) submit annual emission reports that are publicly accessible. The E-PRTR provides facility-level ground truth for Fluxara's European market, enabling TROPOMI-based attribution validation against reported emissions from North Sea operators, Caspian pipeline infrastructure, and EU refineries.

8. ASSESSMENT OF THE 86% ACCURACY AND 14% MAE CLAIM
8.1 Benchmarking Against the Literature
Fluxara's claimed 86% accuracy (with 14% MAE) versus the STILT physics baseline must be understood as: the Fluxara neural network produces emission attributions that agree with STILT-computed attributions on the same inputs to within 14% mean absolute error across validation facilities, achieving agreement in the correct identification of the highest-emitting facility in a basin-scale scene 86% of the time.
This performance is consistent with, and in several respects superior to, analogous published systems:

CNN-based flux quantification on LES-generated synthetic plumes achieved mean absolute percentage errors of 2.15% on training data, 5.02% on validation data, and 4.84% on test data for source flux rates in kg/hr. The error of predicting source flux rates using conventional flux inversion methods is around 25–50%. However, this study used high-resolution AVIRIS-NG analogue data (3–5 m pixels), not the 5.5 × 7 km TROPOMI grid, explaining why its absolute errors are lower. ScienceDirect
Joyce et al. (2023) on PRISMA data achieved emission quantification within the range of precision between 30–90% depending on atmospheric conditions, with their neural approach systematically outperforming conventional methods.
FootNet (Dadheech et al., 2025) shows posterior flux agreement with STILT that implies attribution accuracy broadly consistent with 80–90% agreement at the facility level.

Fluxara's claimed 14% MAE is plausible for TROPOMI-resolution attribution where the 5.5 × 7 km pixel significantly constrains sub-pixel source localisation precision, and where ERA5 wind speed uncertainty (approximately ±10–15% at boundary layer heights over flat terrain) constitutes an irreducible error floor.
8.2 Honest Caveats and Scientific Risk Areas
Wind speed uncertainty: The dominant source of error in any satellite methane attribution system is wind speed uncertainty. ERA5 winds carry approximately ±10–15% uncertainty at relevant heights over flat terrain. This propagates directly into emission rate estimates and sets a hard lower bound on achievable MAE regardless of neural network sophistication.
TROPOMI pixel size: At 5.5 × 7 km, TROPOMI cannot resolve sub-pixel source locations within an industrial facility cluster. Fluxara's attribution relies on the EPA GHGRP and E-PRTR facility databases to assign observed enhancements to the correct facility when multiple facilities fall within a single TROPOMI pixel.
Cloud cover and aerosol masking: TROPOMI's observational coverage is zero over tropical regions and high latitudes during certain seasons, with cloud cover being the primary limiting factor. For Fluxara's target geographies (US oil basins, North Sea), valid-observation days are typically 40–60% of days annually, meaning attribution is not achievable every day at every facility. nih
Training data: Fluxara's neural network will initially be trained on synthetic plumes generated by the Gaussian plume formulas and on STILT outputs. Performance claims will require validation against independent ground truth (controlled releases, aircraft mass balance measurements) during the prototype phase.

9. ASSESSMENT OF SCIENTIFIC NOVELTY AND DIFFERENTIATION
The scientific novelty of Fluxara lies in the combination of four elements that have been individually demonstrated but not integrated at production scale:

Real-time inference from free TROPOMI data — using publicly available daily observations rather than requiring commercial tasked satellite acquisitions
Neural surrogate for the full attribution pipeline — replacing STILT rather than augmenting it, eliminating the HPC dependency entirely
Bayesian uncertainty quantification via MC Dropout — providing calibrated confidence intervals on emission rate attributions suitable for regulatory reporting

Monte Carlo Dropout was introduced by Gal and Ghahramani (2016) as a Bayesian approximation method to quantify model uncertainty in deep learning. The core idea is to interpret dropout from a Bayesian perspective: dropout can be seen as a practical way to approximate Bayesian inference in deep networks, enabling the model to express uncertainty in its predictions through multiple forward passes at inference time. arxiv
Applied to Fluxara, MC Dropout provides a principled, computationally tractable mechanism for generating uncertainty bounds on each emission attribution — a capability absent from conventional STILT inversions, which produce point estimates without propagating model uncertainty.

Direct output to regulatory databases — the system is architected to output against EPA GHGRP and E-PRTR facility registries rather than producing raw spatial emission fields requiring additional expert interpretation


10. CONCLUSION
The scientific foundations of Fluxara are strong. The approach is grounded in established atmospheric physics (Gaussian plume mass balance, Lagrangian transport theory), implements a neural architecture (U-Net) that has been independently validated by at least five peer-reviewed groups for methane plume detection and quantification from satellite imagery, uses the best available global reanalysis wind product (ERA5) as the standard input in the field, and targets a gap — real-time facility-level attribution from free TROPOMI data — that is confirmed as unaddressed by the peer-reviewed literature and commercial landscape alike.
The 86% accuracy and 14% MAE claims are scientifically credible relative to the benchmarks available in the literature for TROPOMI-resolution inversion. They will require empirical validation against independent ground truth measurements during the prototype phase, and Fluxara's roadmap appropriately schedules this validation before commercial deployment. The 1,801× speedup over STILT is the primary technical claim and is consistent with the 650× speedup demonstrated by FootNet and the additional computational gains achievable through an architecture that eliminates the post-footprint optimisation step.
